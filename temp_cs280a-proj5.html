Part 0: Setup

Play with the Model using Text Prompts

DeepFloyd conditions on 4096-dimensional prompt embeddings instead of raw text. Given a prompt pp, a frozen encoder fθf_{\theta}​ maps it to

𝐞=fθ(p)∈ℝ4096,\mathbf{e} = f_{\theta}(p) \in \mathbb{R}^{4096},

and e\mathbf{e} is used as the conditioning signal for the diffusion model. I generated these embeddings once on the provided HuggingFace cluster, saved them as a .pth file, and loaded them in Colab for all later experiments.

I used three prompts that share a cohesive “cute, slightly surreal” style while varying scene complexity:

  • “a tiny-fat shiba inu trying to hide inside a donut”
  • “a shiba inu getting married with chocolate cake”
  • “a cute orange cat playing a ball”

For each of these prompts, I used the same random seed (seed = 111) and generated images with two different values of num_inference_steps: 50 and 100. In both cases I produced three samples per prompt.

num_inference_steps = 50：

num_inference_steps = 100：

Across the two settings, I observed that increasing num_inference_steps from 50 to 100 generally improved image quality, but the effect depended on how “realistic” the prompt was.
For the shiba inu in a donut and the orange cat playing a ball, the 100-step samples look noticeably better: shapes are cleaner, lighting is smoother, and the compositions are more consistent with the text (the dog is clearly inside the donut, and the cat–ball interaction is more natural). These prompts are close to real-world photos, so additional refinement steps mainly enhance realism and detail.

In contrast, the “shiba inu getting married with chocolate cake” prompt remains less successful even with 100 steps. The model sometimes mixes humans, dogs, and the cake in a confusing way, and even switches to a cartoon style. This suggests that when the text describes a very unrealistic or ambiguous situation, simply increasing num_inference_steps cannot fully resolve the semantic difficulty: the model captures “wedding” and “cake” but struggles to produce a coherent, believable scene that matches the exact prompt.

Part 1: Sampling Loops

1.1 Implementing the Forward Process

To implement the forward process of a diffusion model, I take a clean image (x_0) and add Gaussian noise according to the schedule provided by alphas_cumprod. The forward process is

q(xt|x0)=𝒩(xt;α‾t,x0,,(1−α‾t)𝐈)q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\bigl(x_t;\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t},x_0,,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\bigr)

which is equivalent to the reparameterization

xt=α‾t,x0+1−α‾t,ϵ,ϵ∼𝒩(0,𝐈).x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t},x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t},\epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}).

In code, for a given timestep t, I read alpha_bar = alphas_cumprod[t], sample ϵ with torch.randn_like(x0), and compute x_t using the above formula. I apply this function to the Campanile image at timesteps t∈{250,500,750}t \in \{250, 500, 750\}. As t increases, the images become progressively more noisy and the original structure gradually disappears, matching the expected behavior of the forward diffusion process.

x_t = torch.sqrt(alphas_cumprod[t]) * im + torch.sqrt(1 - alphas_cumprod[t]) * torch.randn_like(im)

.

1.2 Classical Denoising

To denoise the three noisy Campanile images at timesteps t∈{250,500,750}t \in \{250, 500, 750\}, I applied Gaussian blur filters using torchvision.transforms.functional.gaussian_blur with increasing kernel sizes:

im_denoise_250 = TF.gaussian_blur(im_noisy_250, 3)
im_denoise_500 = TF.gaussian_blur(im_noisy_500, 5)
im_denoise_750 = TF.gaussian_blur(im_noisy_750, 7)

Gaussian blur removes high-frequency noise but also destroys fine details and edges. For t=250, the denoised image is smoother but still recognizable; at t=500 and especially t=750, the strong blur is needed to suppress the noise, yet the result becomes overly smooth and washed out, with many structures irreversibly lost. This shows that simple classical filtering cannot reliably invert the forward diffusion process once heavy noise has been added.

.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

Original image:

Noisy and denoised images:

In this part, I use the pretrained DeepFloyd UNet (stage_1.unet) to perform one-step denoising on the noisy Campanile images at timesteps t∈{250,500,750}t \in \{250, 500, 750\}. The UNet is conditioned on a null text embedding corresponding to the prompt “a high quality picture”.

Given a noisy image xtx_t and timestep tt, the UNet predicts the noise ϵ^=ϵϕ(xt,t,c)\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\phi(x_t, t, c), where cc is the text embedding. Using the forward diffusion equation

xt=α‾tx0+1−α‾tϵx_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 – \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon

I invert it to obtain an estimate of the clean image:

x^0=xt−1−α‾tϵ^α‾t\hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t – \sqrt{1 – \bar{\alpha}_t}\,\hat{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}

Compared with classical Gaussian blur, the one-step diffusion denoiser recovers much sharper edges and more realistic colors. For t=250t=250, the reconstructed image is close to the original; for t=500t=500 and t=750t=750, some artifacts remain but the global structure is still far better than the heavily smoothed Gaussian-denoised results. This illustrates that a learned diffusion model can more effectively invert the noising process than simple low-pass filtering.

.

1.4 Iterative Denoising

To implement iterative denoising, I constructed a strided list of timesteps

{t0,t1,…,tK}={990,960,…,0}\{t_0, t_1, \dots, t_K\} = \{990, 960, \dots, 0\} 
strided_timesteps = list(range(0, 1000, 30))
strided_timesteps = strided_timesteps[::-1]

by starting at 990 and decreasing with a stride of 30. At denoising step ii, I am at timestep t=tit = t_i with image xtx_t, and I want to move to a less noisy timestep t′=ti+1t’ = t_{i+1}.

For each step, I first estimate the clean image x0x_0​ from xtx_t​ using the one-step denoising formula. Then, following the DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model) update, I compute the next (less noisy) latent xt′x_{t’} as

xt′=α‾t′βt1−α‾tx^0+αt(1−α‾t′)1−α‾txt+vσx_{t’} = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t’}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\hat{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\bigl(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t’}\bigr)}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_t + v_\sigma

where

αt=α‾tα‾t′,βt=1−αt\alpha_t = \frac{\bar{\alpha}_t}{\bar{\alpha}_{t’}}, \quad \beta_t = 1 – \alpha_t

and vσv_\sigma is additional random noise added by the provided add_variance function.

1.5 Diffusion Model Sampling

In this part, I use the diffusion model to generate images from pure noise. I first sample a random tensor as the initial noisy image and then run the iterative_denoise function with i_start = 0 along the strided timestep schedule, conditioning on the prompt embedding for “a high quality picture”. With the same random seed, I generate five different samples.

The results look like low-resolution natural photos: the colors and rough structures are reasonable, but the images are still blurry and contain noticeable artifacts. This matches the expectation that, without classifier-free guidance, the model can push samples toward the natural image manifold but cannot produce very sharp or highly detailed images.

.

1.6 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

To improve image quality, I replace the original iterative denoising with a classifier-free guidance (CFG) version. At each denoising step, I run the UNet twice on the same latent xtx_t​: once with the conditional prompt embedding (“a high quality picture”) and once with an unconditional (empty) prompt embedding. These give a conditional noise estimate ϵc\epsilon_c and an unconditional estimate ϵu\epsilon_u. I then combine them as

ϵcfg=ϵu+γ(ϵc−ϵu),\epsilon_{\mathrm{cfg}} = \epsilon_{u} + \gamma \bigl(\epsilon_{c} – \epsilon_{u}\bigr),

with guidance scale γ=7\gamma = 7. The rest of the DDPM update is identical to the previous iterative denoising step.

# combine conditional and unconditional noise estimates
noise_est, predicted_variance = torch.split(model_output, image.shape[1], dim=1)
uncond_noise_est, _ = torch.split(uncond_model_output, image.shape[1], dim=1)
noise_est = uncond_noise_est + scale * (noise_est - uncond_noise_est)

Using CFG and sampling from pure noise with the same seed, the generated images are noticeably sharper and more coherent than those without CFG: shapes and edges are clearer and the overall appearance is closer to natural photographs, although the diversity of samples is somewhat reduced and the colors look less appealing.

.

1.7 Image-to-image Translation

To perform image editing, I start from an input image, add a moderate amount of noise, and then denoise it using the CFG-based iterative sampler. Concretely, I take the original Campanile image, apply the forward process to reach different noise levels corresponding to starting indices istart∈{1,3,5,7,10,20}i_{\text{start}} \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 20\} on the strided timestep schedule, and then run iterative_denoise_cfg with the prompt “a high quality picture”.

When istarti_{\text{start}} is small (e.g., 1 or 3), the corresponding timestep is large, so the image is heavily noised. After denoising, the results still roughly follow the Campanile layout, but textures, colors, and background can change noticeably, giving more “creative” edits. As istarti_{\text{start}} becomes larger (e.g., 10 or 20), the starting noise level is lower and the edited images stay much closer to the original photo, mainly refining contrast and sharpness rather than altering the scene.

I observe the same trend on two of my own test images: low istarti_{\text{start}}​ produces strong, semantic changes, while high istarti_{\text{start}}behaves more like gentle retouching.

1.7.2 Inpainting

For inpainting, I reuse the CFG-based iterative denoising loop, but at every step I replace the known (unmasked) region with the original image plus the correct amount of noise using the given binary mask.

xt←𝐦xt+(1−𝐦)forward(xorig,t)x_t \leftarrow \mathbf{m}\,x_t + (1-\mathbf{m})\,\mathrm{forward}(x_{\text{orig}}, t) 
orig_noisy_prev = forward(original_image, prev_t)
image = mask * pred_prev_image + (1 - mask) * orig_noisy_prev

I first inpaint the top of the Campanile image and then apply the same procedure to two of my own images, successfully filling the masked regions with plausible new content while keeping the rest of the image unchanged.

1.8 Visual Anagrams

In the visual anagrams experiment, I modify the CFG-based sampler to use two different text prompts, one for the upright image and one for the upside-down image. The process is:

  1. Compute DDPM coefficients
  2. CFG noise for upright image (prompt p1)
  3. CFG noise for flipped image (prompt p2)
  4. Combine guidance and take one DDPM step

At each denoising step, I run the UNet with CFG on the current latent xtx_t​ using prompt p1p_1 to get a noise estimate ϵ1\epsilon_1​, and on the vertically flipped latent flip(xt)\text{flip}(x_t) with prompt p2p_2 to get ϵ2\epsilon_2. I then flip ϵ2\epsilon_2 back, average the two noise estimates, and use this combined noise to perform the DDPM update.

This produces images that look like one concept when viewed normally but reveal a different concept when flipped upside down. In my first illusion, my prompts are “a large, soft shiba inu floating in the sky like a balloon” for the upright direction and “a cute orange cat doing gymnastics” for the flipped image.

In my second illusion, my prompts are “a photo of a Chihuahua” for the upright direction and “a photo of a blueberry muffin” for the flipped image (This works really well!!).

.

1.9 Hybrid Images

In the hybrid images experiment, I modify the CFG-based sampler to fuse low-frequency information from one prompt and high-frequency information from another prompt. The process is:

  1. Compute DDPM coefficients
  2. Get CFG noise for prompt p1​ (base image)
  3. Get CFG noise for prompt p2​ (detail image)
  4. Apply a Gaussian blur to extract a low-pass version of ϵ1​ and a high-pass version of ϵ2​
  5. Combine them into a hybrid noise map and take one DDPM step

At each timestep tt, I run the UNet with CFG under prompt p1p_1 to obtain ϵ1\epsilon_1, and under prompt p2p_2​ to obtain ϵ2\epsilon_2. I then apply a Gaussian blur (kernel size 3, σ=2\sigma=2) to extract low frequencies from ϵ1\epsilon_1​ and high frequencies from ϵ2\epsilon_2​, and sum them to get the final noise estimate. This fused noise is plugged into the usual DDPM update, so the resulting image looks like the first prompt from far away (dominated by low frequencies) and like the second prompt up close (dominated by high-frequency details).

Part 2: Bells & Whistles

More visual anagrams

To build more general visual anagrams, I introduce a pair of helper functions, apply_transform and invert_transform, that can apply and undo several spatial transformations on the image tensor. The function takes an image x and applies a geometric transformation specified by mode, such as vertical flip, transpose, or rotations by 90∘,180∘,90^\circ, 180^\circ,or 270∘270^\circ.

The companion function returns the inverse transformation: for self-inverse operations (flip, transpose, 180° rotation) it simply re-applies the same mode, while 90° and 270° rotations are inverted by rotating in the opposite direction. This allows me to transform the latent, run the diffusion model under a second prompt, and then map the result back to the original orientation.

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch!

Part 1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

1.1 Implementing the UNet

Following the figures, I implemented the denoiser as class UnconditionalUNet(nn.Module), assembling the network from the provided Conv, DownConv, UpConv, Flatten/Unflatten, Concat, and the composed ConvBlock/DownBlock/UpBlock modules to match the architecture shown in the diagrams.

1.2 Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

To generate noisy training pairs, I implemented

ϵ∼𝒩(0,I),zi=min⁡(1,max⁡(0,xi+σϵi)). \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad z_i = \min\bigl(1,\; \max(0,\; x_i + \sigma \epsilon_i)\bigr).

which adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ to the clean MNIST digit and clamps the result to [0,1][0,1].

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

2.1 Adding Time Conditioning to UNet

In code, I implement an FCBlock(nn.Module) and TimeConditionalFM(nn.Module) using the structure in the following figure that takes the scalar timestep tt and maps it through a small MLP to a D-dimensional embedding, which is then broadcast and added to the feature maps at the 7×77\times7 and 14×1414\times14 layers of the UNet (as in the red box in the diagram). The helper function time_fm_forward samples x0,x1,tx_0, x_1, t, computes the interpolated state xtx_t​, and feeds (xt,t)(x_t, t) into the time-conditioned UNet for training.

.

2.2 Training the UNet

I trained the UNet with the following process, and used  an exponential learning rate decay scheduler with a gamma of 0.1:

  1. Sample a clean image x1x_1 from the training set.
  2. Pick a random time t∼Uniform(0,1)t \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1).
  3. Sample pure noise x0∼𝒩(0,I)x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, I).
  4. Mix noise and image to get a point on the path: xt=(1−t)x0+tx1x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1
  5. Ask the network uθ(xt,t)u_\theta(x_t, t) to predict the “velocity” x1−x0x_1 – x_0.
  6. Update θ\theta by gradient descent to minimize |(x1−x0)−uθ(xt,t)|2.| (x_1 – x_0) – u_\theta(x_t, t) |^2.
  7. Repeat for many images until the network has learned the velocity field.

The hyperparameters I used are:

batch_size = 64
learning_rate = 1e-2
num_ts=50
hidden_dim = 64
num_epochs = 10

2.3 Sampling from the UNet

I applied the sampling function using the following algorithm:

  1. Start from pure noise: x0∼𝒩(0,I)x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, I)
  2. Walk along the learned flow
    • For TT small steps, let t=0,1T,…,1t = 0, \frac{1}{T}, \dots, 1.
    • At each step, update xt​←xt​+1T​uθ​(xt​,t),x_t​←x_t​+\frac{1} {T} ​uθ​(xt​,t),
      • i.e., move a little in the velocity direction predicted by the network.
  3. Return the final point
    • The last xtx_t​ (at t=1t=1) is the generated sample that should look like a real image.

2.4 Adding Class-Conditioning to UNet

To add class-conditioning to the time-dependent UNet, I extend the architecture to take both a timestep tt and a digit class c∈{0,…,9}c\in\{0,\dots,9\}. The class label cc is first converted to a one-hot vector and, following the provided template, I pass tt and the one-hot class vector through four separate FCBlocks (fc1_t, fc2_t for time; fc1_c, fc2_c for class) to obtain channel-wise embeddings. These embeddings are then broadcast over the spatial dimensions and used to modulate the decoder feature maps at the 7×77\times 7 and 14×1414\times 14 resolutions via

feat7×7′=c1⊙feat7×7+t1,feat14×14′=c2⊙feat14×14+t2,\text{feat}_{7\times7}’ = c_1 \odot \text{feat}_{7\times7} + t_1,\qquad \text{feat}_{14\times14}’ = c_2 \odot \text{feat}_{14\times14} + t_2,

where c1,c2,t1,t2c_1,c_2,t_1,t_2​ are the outputs of the corresponding FCBlocks. I also implement classifier-free guidance by multiplying the one-hot class vector with a binary mask, so that with probability puncondp_{\text{uncond}}​ the class embedding is dropped (set to zero) and the network is trained in an unconditional mode as well.

.

2.5 Training the UNet

I applied the below process to train the class-conditioning UNet:

  1. Sample an image and its label
  2. Drop the label with probability puncondp_{\text{uncond}}​, replace cc by the zero vector.
  3. Sample time and noise
    • Draw t∼Uniform(0,1)t \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1) and x0∼N(0,I)x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, I).
    • Form the mixed point xt=(1−t)x0+tx1.x_t = (1 – t)x_0 + t x_1.
  4. Train the conditional velocity field
    • Let the network predict uθ(xt,t,c)u_\theta(x_t, t, c).
    • Do gradient descent on ∥(x1−x0)−uθ(xt,t,c)∥2,\|(x_1 – x_0) – u_\theta(x_t, t, c)\|^2,and repeat until convergence.

The hyperparameters I used are:

batch_size = 64
learning_rate = 1e-2

hidden_dim = 64
num_epochs = 10

2.6 Sampling from the UNet

With the sampling algorithm below, I finally got some good looking digits:

  1. Input a one-hot class vector cc and a guidance scale γ\gamma.
  2. Start from noise x0∼N(0,I)x_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, I) and set xt=x0x_t = x_0.
  3. For TT time steps t=0,1T,…,1t = 0, \tfrac{1}{T}, \dots, 1, follow the flow with guidance:
    • Compute unconditional velocity uuncond​=uθ​(xt​,t,0)u_{uncond}​=u_θ​(x_t​,t,0)
    • Compute conditional velocity ucond​=uθ​(xt​,t,c).u_{cond}​=u_θ​(x_t​,t,c).
    • Combine them with classifier-free guidance u=uuncond​+γ(ucond​−uuncond​).u=u_{uncond}​+γ(u_{cond}​−u_{uncond}​).
    • Take an Euler step xt←xt+1Tu.x_t \leftarrow x_t + \frac{1}{T} \, u .
  4. The last xtx_t is a generated image biased toward class cc, with strength controlled by γ\gamma.
